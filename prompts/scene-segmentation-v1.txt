You are annotating literary scene boundaries in a unitized book.

Your task is to identify:
1. where one coherent literary scene ends and another begins; and
2. meaningful boundaries involving document/non-narrative material.

The text has already been divided into ordered units. Each unit has a stable ID such as [000123].

INPUT REGIONS

The user message contains three ordered regions:

PAST
Context before the region being annotated.

TARGET
The only region in which you may place boundaries.

FUTURE
Context after the region being annotated.

PAST and FUTURE are context only. Use them to understand transitions involving TARGET, but never return an ID from PAST or FUTURE.

A boundary before the first TARGET unit is allowed when the transition from PAST into that TARGET unit is genuinely a boundary.

Every predicted boundary must therefore use the ID of a unit in TARGET.

LITERARY SCENES

A scene is a contiguous stretch of narrative forming one coherent dramatic or narrative situation.

The primary question is:

Has the narrative meaningfully left one coherent situation and begun another?

Evidence for a new scene can include meaningful changes in:

- story time
- location
- point of view or focalization
- principal characters
- narrative thread
- immediate goal
- conflict
- dramatic situation

No single factor automatically creates a new scene.

One strong discontinuity may be sufficient. Several small changes may still belong to one continuous scene.

Judge the overall narrative situation rather than counting changed attributes.

Prefer continuity when the same action, conversation, pursuit, conflict, goal, or dramatic situation clearly continues.

CHANGES THAT OFTEN SUPPORT A NEW SCENE

A new scene is likely when the narrative makes a substantial transition such as:

- a meaningful jump forward or backward in story time
- a move to a different location accompanied by a distinct narrative situation
- a switch to another point of view or focal character that begins a separately occurring situation
- a switch to another narrative thread
- the clear conclusion of one dramatic situation followed by a distinct new one
- a cut to a substantially separate group of characters
- entering a sustained flashback, dream, memory, vision, or embedded narrative that functions as its own scene
- returning from such a sustained embedded scene to the surrounding narrative
- a chapter or section transition where the underlying narrative situation genuinely changes

WHAT NORMALLY DOES NOT CREATE A NEW SCENE

Do not create a new scene merely because of:

- a new paragraph
- a new speaker
- a character entering or leaving
- a new conversational topic
- a new action beat within the same continuing situation
- an emotional change
- a change in immediate goal that remains part of the same ongoing situation
- brief introspection
- a brief memory, flashback, dream, vision, or recollection
- a momentary observation of another character's perspective or reaction
- a small passage of time
- movement between nearby locations, such as one room to another or through a hallway
- ordinary transit or travel while the same narrative situation continues
- a short transitional or summary passage connecting surrounding action
- a chapter or section heading when the same scene continues across it

These features can contribute evidence for a boundary, but none is automatically sufficient.

Always ask whether the narrative actually leaves one coherent dramatic or narrative situation and establishes another.

SPECIAL CASES

Location:
Movement alone is not a scene change. Moving from one room to another, walking through a building, travelling toward an immediate goal, or otherwise changing physical position can remain one scene when the dramatic situation continues.

Time:
A brief passage of time is not automatically a new scene. A substantial time discontinuity that resets the active situation strongly supports a new scene even if the location or characters remain the same.

Characters:
A character entering or leaving is not automatically a new scene. A cut to a separately occurring group of characters or narrative thread usually is.

Point of view:
Do not treat ordinary description of another character's actions, appearance, or reaction as a POV change. A genuine switch in focalization or viewpoint that begins a separately occurring narrative situation strongly supports a boundary.

Flashbacks, memories, dreams, visions, letters, and embedded accounts:
These are not automatically separate scenes.

A brief recollection, quotation, summary, letter passage, dream fragment, or imagined event can remain embedded in the surrounding scene.

If the narrative substantially enters the embedded material and presents it as its own sustained coherent dramatic situation, treat it as a scene. Place a boundary when entering it and another when returning to the surrounding narrative, when those transitions occur in TARGET.

Aftermath:
The immediate consequences of an event can remain part of the same scene. For example, a fight ending followed immediately by treatment of injuries or discussion of what just happened is not automatically a new scene.

A boundary is appropriate when the previous dramatic situation has genuinely concluded and the narrative establishes a distinct new situation.

BOUNDARY PLACEMENT

Represent a boundary using the unit ID of the first unit belonging to the new scene or document region.

Do not return the final unit of the old scene.

Chapter and section headings attach to the material that follows them.

Therefore, when a genuine new literary scene begins at a chapter or section transition, place the boundary before the heading itself.

A chapter or section heading does not create a boundary by itself.

Do not place a boundary before the first unit of the book merely because it begins the first scene. Boundaries represent transitions between regions.

DOCUMENT / NON-NARRATIVE MATERIAL

Books may contain material outside the narrative scene sequence.

Examples can include:

- copyright and publication information
- tables of contents
- dedications
- non-narrative prefaces or forewords
- acknowledgements
- appendices
- indexes
- bibliographies
- promotional or publisher material
- other material serving a document-level rather than narrative function

These examples are illustrative, not an exhaustive list.

Determine whether something is document/non-narrative material from its function in the book, not merely from particular words, formatting, or headings.

A document boundary marks a meaningful transition:

- from one coherent document/non-narrative region to a different coherent document/non-narrative region; or
- between document/non-narrative material and narrative material.

Do not create a document boundary for every line, entry, heading, or item within one coherent document region.

For example, the individual entries of one table of contents belong to the same document region and should not each receive boundaries.

Ordinary narrative chapter and section headings are not document boundaries merely because they are structural.

A prologue or epilogue that contains narrative scenes is narrative material, not document material merely because it is called "Prologue" or "Epilogue."

When transitioning from document material into the narrative, such as from a dedication into the opening narrative chapter, classify the transition as a document boundary.

When transitioning from narrative into document material, classify it as a document boundary.

DOCUMENT VS LITERARY BOUNDARIES

The output uses two lists.

"boundaries_before" contains every predicted boundary in TARGET:
- literary scene boundaries
- document boundaries

"document_boundaries_before" contains only document boundaries.

Every ID in "document_boundaries_before" must therefore also appear in "boundaries_before".

Conceptually:

literary scene boundaries =
boundaries_before minus document_boundaries_before

If a transition involves entering or leaving document/non-narrative material, classify it as a document boundary rather than as an ordinary literary scene boundary.
